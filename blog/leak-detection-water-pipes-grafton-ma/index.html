Signs of a hidden leak

A pipe leak hidden in a wall, slab or yard rarely announces itself. The signs are indirect. A water bill that climbs without a change in habits, a meter that moves when everything is off, damp or warm floor spots, musty smells, peeling paint, or the sound of running water in a quiet house all point to a leak.

  • Higher water bills with no change in use.
  • Meter dial spinning when all water is off.
  • Warm patch on a floor, which can indicate a hot line leak.
  • Stained ceilings or bubbling paint.
  • Low water pressure that appeared suddenly.
  • Soggy ground in the yard along the service line.

The meter test

Turn off every tap and appliance, including the ice maker. Look at your water meter and note the reading or the small low-flow indicator. Wait an hour without using water and look again. Movement means water is going somewhere. If the meter stops when you close the main valve at the house, the leak is inside. If it keeps moving, the leak is between the meter and the house.

Checking the easy sources first

  • Toilets: add food coloring to the tank and wait fifteen minutes.
  • Faucets and shutoff valves under sinks.
  • Water heater and its relief valve discharge.
  • Outdoor spigots and irrigation lines.
  • Appliance supply lines for washers and ice makers.

Most leaks are at fixtures and connections, not buried pipe. Fix those first, then retest the meter. If the meter still moves, the leak is hidden.

How professionals find hidden leaks

Acoustic listening equipment hears the sound of water escaping a pressurized pipe. Thermal imaging shows temperature differences from hot leaks or wet materials. Moisture meters check walls and floors. Pressure tests isolate sections of pipe. Together, they narrow a leak to a small area so we open as little as possible. We find the leak and describe the repair before cutting anything. If the line has failed in several places, repiping may be the more sensible answer.

Act fast, even on a slow leak

Slow leaks rot framing, grow mold and drive up bills. Shut the water off to the affected area if you can, keep records of the meter test, and call. Insurance generally covers sudden damage but not long-term seepage, so early action also protects a future claim.

Hot water lines, slab leaks and radiant heat

Leaks in hot water lines under a slab can warm the floor above them. A hot spot under your feet, a bill that rises in winter and a water heater that runs constantly point toward a hot line leak. Radiant heat tubing can leak too and drop system pressure. Detection tools can trace these lines without breaking the floor first, which keeps repairs small.

Documenting for insurance

Record the meter reading before and after your test, take dated photographs of stains and keep any plumber report on the cause. Insurers distinguish sudden damage from long-term leakage, so early documentation helps. Ask your carrier before major repairs about what proof they need, and whether they cover tear-out and access work.

Leaks outdoors and in the yard

Not all leaks are inside the house. A broken service line, an irrigation line or an outdoor spigot can waste thousands of gallons without leaving a stain. Look for unusually green strips of lawn, soft ground, a patch of grass that stays wet in dry weather or the sound of water near the meter. Shut off the irrigation system and test the meter again to separate lawn water from house water.

Smart sensors and shutoff valves

Inexpensive water sensors placed near the water heater, washing machine, under sinks and in the basement send an alert to your phone at the first drop. Automatic shutoff valves close the main when they detect a leak or a flow pattern that looks wrong. They are a good fit for homes left empty for long stretches. A plumber can install one on your main line and test it.

What a leak detection visit looks like

The plumber starts with questions, then checks the meter and fixtures. Listening equipment and thermal images narrow the search. Pressure tests isolate sections of pipe. Only then does anyone open a wall, and usually only a small patch.

You receive a clear report of what was found and what it will take to fix. If the leak is inexpensive to repair, we do it. If it signals a failing system, we say so and explain the options. Either way, you will know what you are dealing with.

Why speed matters

Water finds its way through framing and insulation. A small leak found in a week costs a patch. The same leak found in a year can mean rot, mold and a ceiling replacement. Testing the meter takes an hour and settles the question.

Keep a baseline

Read your meter on the first of the month for a few months and write it down. A baseline tells you what normal looks like, so a jump is obvious. Many leaks are found because someone noticed a number that did not fit.

What to do first when you find a leak

Close the main shutoff, open a low tap to drain the lines, keep clear of wet electrical items and photograph the damage. If you smell gas, leave and call from outside. Then call a plumber and describe what you saw. Do not wait to see whether it dries.

Local note

Water meters, districts and Grafton

Water service in Grafton comes from the Grafton Water District, the South Grafton Water District or a private well. Ask your district how to read your meter, who owns the pipe up to the meter and who owns the line past it. Homes on private wells have no meter, so a pressure tank that cycles constantly is the usual clue to a leak.

Plumbing repairs are governed by the Massachusetts code, 248 CMR 10.00, and performed by licensed plumbers. Call 811 before any excavation to locate buried utilities.

Common questions

How can I tell if I have a hidden water leak?

Check the meter with all water off. If it moves, water is escaping.

Does a high water bill always mean a leak?

No, but a sudden unexplained rise is a good reason to test.

Can a toilet leak without sound?

Yes. Dye in the tank shows silent leaks.

Can you find a leak without opening walls?

Often we narrow it with listening and thermal tools and open only a small area.

Who owns the water line between the meter and my Grafton house?

Usually the homeowner owns the line from the meter to the house. Confirm with your water district.
