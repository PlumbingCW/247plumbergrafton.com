Business plumbing emergency: first steps

The first call is to the plumber. The second is to your insurer. Shut off the water at the nearest valve, protect equipment and stock, and move people away from wet floors. Photograph everything. Keep receipts.

  • Locate and close the main or zone valve.
  • Switch off power to wet equipment.
  • Move inventory off the floor.
  • Tell us if sewage is involved, since it affects the response.

Working with landlords and property managers

Property managers want clear reports. We describe the cause, the repair and what to watch, in language you can pass to an owner or a tenant. When the same unit calls repeatedly, we flag the pattern. Many multi-family issues trace to shared drain lines, so sewer inspection pays off.

Scheduling matters. We aim for work outside business hours when possible, and we tell you in advance what will be shut off and for how long.

Restaurants, grease and floor drains

Food service is the heaviest user of drains in most small commercial districts. Grease and starch coat the inside of lines and harden, and a floor drain that smells or drains slowly at the end of a busy night is already warning you.

A grease trap that is not emptied on schedule overflows into the line behind it. We clear the blockage first, then talk about a cleaning routine so the same call does not repeat during dinner service.

A hand sink that runs lukewarm, a three-compartment sink that drains slowly and a mop sink that backs up are all common after-hours calls. Tell the dispatcher which fixtures are affected so the right equipment arrives on the first trip.

Landlords, tenants and who pays

In shared buildings, the lease decides who pays for a plumbing repair, and the cause often decides it. A tenant who flushed a rag may owe the repair. A failed shared stack usually falls to the owner. We document the cause in plain terms and take photographs, so the people who must settle the bill have facts in front of them. We do not take sides, but we do write down what we found.

  • Report the problem as soon as it starts to limit damage.
  • Ask the plumber for a written description of the cause.
  • Keep photographs and receipts.
  • Notify your insurer if water has damaged inventory or equipment.

Planning for the next emergency

Know where the shutoffs are before something fails. Label the main, the water heater supply and any zone valves, and make sure staff on a closing shift can find them. A thirty second plan in writing prevents an hour of water. Exercise the valves once a year, because a main valve that has not moved in a decade often will not close when you need it.
